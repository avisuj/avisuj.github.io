Worden names four tasks of grief: accept the reality of the loss, process the pain, adjust to a world without what you hoped for, find an enduring connection while moving forward. I've been using it this week to structure something harder than I expected to write.

## Accept the reality of the loss

Love was conditional in my house. I didn't see it as a child, I just adapted to it. If love has a price, you learn to pay it, and I paid it in performance. Good grades were currency. Obedience was currency. Later, so was agreeing to whatever kept the peace.

I was raised in a fixed mindset family while wired for a growth one. That's a strange combination to grow up inside. My parents needed me to already be the finished product, and I needed room to become one. So I filled the gap with justification and performance, over and over, until it was reflexive.

That reflex cost me more than I want to write down. When someone close to our family raised a concern about Abby, and a doctor had to check, my first move was to defend rather than get curious. Not to find out what happened. Defend. I had spent my whole life protecting my family from criticism, and the habit was so deep it fired even there, even then.

I lost her innocence. The girl who loved to cuddle and wanted to roughhouse stopped being comfortable being touched, and somewhere in that same window I was busy managing my father's reputation instead of my daughter's safety.

The performing didn't stop at defending. It showed up everywhere. My parents wanted things adjusted, and my family adjusted. Behave. Lose weight. Whatever it took. Yes, Mom, we'll fly to India for Abby's first birthday. Of course I'll pick an Indian middle name. It didn't matter what it cost or who it hurt. I was still performing being their son, decades in.

I carry the anger in my upper back and my neck. Right between the shoulders, like something I've been holding up for a long time.

## Process the pain

It's not only grief. It's shame and guilt for not being who I could have been, and it's the repair I'm still doing to try to help my family. Some days it feels like the dark night of the soul, purgatory and burning light at the same time, something being stripped that needed to go, but the stripping doesn't feel clean while it's happening. Underneath all of it is just being overwhelmed.

I know the difference matters, even if I can't always hold onto it. Grief mourns what happened to me. Guilt answers for what I did or didn't do. Shame says something is wrong with who I am. 

I'm still in this task. I don't think I'm done with it, and I'm trying to make the jump to adjusting before the pain is finished being processed. Maybe that's fine. Maybe both can happen at once.

## Adjust to a world without what you hoped for

I don't get the relationship with my parents I wanted. That's the adjustment. Figuring out what boundaries actually look like, and how I hold filial love and cleaving to my own family at the same time, is the real work now.

The Fourth Commandment says honor your father and mother. It doesn't say obey them into your forties, and it doesn't say give them unlimited access. I think that's actually the whole answer to what do I owe them. Honor, not obedience. Honor, not access.

Adjusting also means finding joy again with my own family, not someday, now. Doing the actions of loving them instead of just intending to. Making them the priority instead of the thing I get to after I've managed everyone else's approval of me. Stopping the escaping. Learning to hold myself, and letting myself be held by the people actually in my house.

## Find an enduring connection while moving forward

I don't think I'm here yet.

I don't think that's a failure. I think it's just true. Some memories are further along than others, and this one, the one with Abby in it, is going to take longer than the rest, and it should.

Almighty Father, 

You gave your Son to a family that didn't understand him either. I bring you the boy who learned to perform instead of be loved, and I bring you the father who defended before he protected. I don't ask you to make the ache go away. I ask you to keep stripping what needs to go, and to hold what's underneath, because I can't hold it alone.
